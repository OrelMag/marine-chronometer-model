Stopping and starting

A detent chronometer does not start by itself. Once its balance swings too little to carry the discharge jewel past the trip spring and unlock the wheel, it stops, and it takes a quick twist of the box to set the balance going again (Sec. III).

Balance locking arm

Train-blocking screw

A jolt

Why the detent was kept to chronometers in gimbals: a sharp jolt can upset it. Against the balance's swing it can check the balance below what unlocks the wheel, and the chronometer sets (stops); with the swing it can carry the discharge jewel round to unlock a second time, and the wheel trips, escaping an extra tooth, so the hands jump half a second. An illustration of those faults, not how a sound Model 21 behaves on its gimbals.

The arm was fitted from 1947 so a chronometer could be shipped with its balance held; the screw keeps the mainspring's power in while the balance and escapement are out for service. With the screw down the balance swings on, unlocking a wheel that cannot turn, and runs down in a minute or two; raise the screw before then and it carries on. Screw it down or lock the arm, then unlock and twist: the hands will have lost the time it stood.

Rate and timing weights

There is no regulator. The rate is set with the weights on the balance rim, each a nut on a screw: the two timing weights at the arm’s ends, or the small vernier weights inside the rim beside them for fine changes. Both of a pair are turned alike, so the balance stays in poise. Out moves their mass further from the staff, which raises the balance’s moment of inertia and slows it: the hands lose.

The manual gives about 40 s a day for a full turn of the timing weights and 2.8 s for the verniers; the model’s thread is set to match, and the moment of inertia comes from the model’s balance and the parts list’s masses. In an overhaul, balance screws and washers bring the rate within 2 minutes a day, then the timing weights within half a second (Ops. 5, 6); a pre-temperature test at 55 and 90 °F follows, and pairs of screws are moved from hole to hole to correct it (Op. 8, Table IV); the verniers make the final changes (Op. 10). The rim has 24 tapped holes, numbered 1 to 13 round each half from the arm’s end, as the adjuster’s numbered balance block numbers them (Fig. 99): the timing weight stands at 1, the vernier at 2, and the screws in 3 to 12, the screws of a pair in the same hole of each half. The standard set is the four pairs a 1941 Model 21 carries; the parts list allows a fifth. Moving a pair along the rim keeps the moment of inertia, so the rate at the mean temperature stays, while the rate in heat changes: the panel gives Table IV’s figure, linear in temperature, and the balance’s own curve on top of it: for the Model 21’s, the small gain in the middle that the factory test card of No. 3390 shows (Sec. IX); for the split bimetallic balance, its middle temperature error, illustrative, the rim’s curl drawn 20 times as far as it goes. The chart gives the Navy test’s three temperature compensation figures and their limits. The model’s clock runs at the new rate, so the hands drift from real time; try 3600×. Zero puts back the weights’ mid-travel, the standard screws and 72.5 °F. The escapement’s settings on the Adjuster’s bench change the rate too, and the panel says by how much; the bench’s own button takes them back. The weights and screws are drawn on the Hamilton balance only.

Rate book

The navigator doesn't set the hands: the chronometer's error is compared with a time signal, daily, and recorded with its rate, and applied to every reading (Sec. III, Setting; Sec. IX, Table I). Error and daily rate in seconds: + fast or gaining, − slow or losing. Here the signal is the master time, which runs with the model; the hands step in half seconds, so the error is read to the nearest half second.

Swing and isochronismThe balance’s swing and rate over a wind

How far the balance swings is set by the train's torque: each swing the impulse gives it what unlocking, the detent's springs and its own friction take, and it settles where they balance, 1⅜ to 1½ turns of motion (Sec. II), 248° to 270° each way. No balance is quite isochronous: here the escapement, pushing it on either side of its dead point, makes a smaller swing lose (Airy; the hairspring taken as isochronous). The fusee keeps the torque, and so the swing and the rate, nearly the same from full wind to run down; while the key turns, the weaker sustaining spring drives, and the swing dips.

The isochronism check is the manual's (Sec. IX): wound, the gain over the next 12 hours against half the gain over 24; the Navy's tolerance is 0.50 s, and the factory test card of No. 3390 reads 0.00. The mainspring's pull is the solved spring's, from its measured free shape; the sustaining spring's strength is a design (a split ring weaker than the drive, banked in running, as the period's chronometers had it); the balance's free run-down (1/e in 67 s) lies inside what its parts give (tools/physics.js).

30-day performance testThe Navy’s 30-day test, as the model runs

The Navy's test before a chronometer was issued (Sec. IX, Performance Testing): thirty days in six periods of five, at 90, 72½, 55, 55, 72½ and 90 °F, the dial error read daily on Hamilton's comparator to 0.01 s, judged against the Bureau of Ships' limits. Here it is run on the model as it stands (weights, screws, balance, adjuster's bench, mainspring), wound at each daily reading, beside the manual's factory card for chronometer No. 3390. Change something and it is run again. The model has no day-to-day scatter, so its rating and recovery figures come from the comparator's rounding alone.

Adjuster's benchThe escapement’s settings, to adjust by hand

The escapement's settings, as the repairman adjusts them (Sec. VIII, Ops. 84–88, 97). Move one and the detent, jewels and roller are rebuilt with it, and the figures are measured against the manual's the way tools/escapement.js measures them. The model runs with them: the balance swings as far as the impulse, less what unlocking takes, carries it (Stopping and starting), and the escapement's rate changes with where they push the balance about its dead point (Rate and timing weights). If a setting would stop the escapement working, or leave the balance swinging too little to keep it going, the bench says why and keeps the last one that works. The last slider is the hairspring's own isochronism, the seconds a day it gains for each 10° more swing: the model takes it as 0, but an adjuster can set the spring against the escapement, which loses at a smaller swing, so that the rate hardly follows the swing (Swing and isochronism; about −0.1).

About this model

Orel Magidish (assisted by Claude Opus 5.5) – September 2026
orelmag@gmail.com · version dev ·

A working 3D model of the Hamilton Model 21 marine chronometer: a two-day fusee chronometer with a spring detent escapement, made in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, for the U.S. Navy from 1941. Every part runs from one clock: the balance beats twice a second, the hands step in half-seconds, and the fusee and chain follow the hours since winding.

Work in progress. In October 2026 Hamilton’s factory drawings of some twenty Model 21 parts, from the NAWCC, and the British Museum’s measurements of its example showed that this model was built about 12 % too small: its millimetres all came from one sale listing’s plate. The model is being rescaled and its drawn parts rebuilt from the drawings; until every part is drawn, treat its sizes as provisional.

Using it

  • Drag to turn; Shift-drag, middle-drag or drag two fingers to pan; scroll or pinch to zoom. The ? button on the model lists every control, for a mouse and for touch.
  • Tap a part to see what it does and its Hamilton part number.
  • Right-click or long-press a part to fade, hide or isolate it; do the same on empty space to bring hidden or isolated parts back.
  • Space bar stops and restarts the movement; keys 1 to 9 pick the views in the order of their buttons. With the model focused (click it, or Tab to it), the arrow keys turn the view, + and − zoom and 0 resets it. With reduced motion set in the system, views change without animation and the walkthrough leaves ship motion off.
  • Under Parts, single out or hide any part by name, including parts too small to tap.
  • The model keeps Greenwich Mean Time, as Navy chronometers were kept; under Time, Keep: Local time switches to your time zone. (The manual has the hands set only when the chronometer is started, the error being recorded instead; here Set the hands works at any time.)
  • Wind (under Winding), with the movement in its case, turns the case over in its gimbals, opens the shield plate and puts the key on, as the manual winds it; Wind with the key turns the key half a turn at a time until the winding stop catches. Under Rate and timing weights, move the balance’s weights and watch the hands gain or lose.
  • It doesn’t start by itself. Run down, or stopped with the balance locking arm or the train-blocking screw (under Stopping and starting), it needs winding and then Twist to start, the quick twist of the box the manual prescribes; the hands lose the time it stood.

How it was made

  • Measured. The balance, fusee and barrel positions were triangulated from two photographs, each with its own fitted camera. The bridge outline, balance cock, setup cover, screws, engraving and damascene direction were traced on the top view; rendered from that photograph’s camera, the model’s key axes land within 0.4 mm of it. The heights of the plates, wheels and bridges come from a side photograph scaled by the pillar plate’s 3.86 mm edge.
  • Solved. The escape wheel sits 9.40 mm from the balance, where the manual’s impulse roller leaves its specified 0.002 in of roller shake. The third wheel’s position and the train’s tooth sizes were solved so that every arbor clears every wheel and the barrel.
  • Checked against the parts list. Every part in the manual’s parts lists (Figs. 106–110) was checked against the model, and the missing ones added: among them the pillar, mounting-ring and dial screws, the endstone caps, the balance’s hub cap and hold-down screws, the balance locking arm and the train-blocking screw. Every screw has its thread, and every part it passes through or screws into has its hole.
  • Checked. The parts were tested for collisions to 0.05 mm through a full escapement cycle, at many positions of the train and from run down to full wind, including the chain, the springs and the barrel, whose clearances were measured as it turns. Every screw, pivot and pawl was checked for proper seating and engagement. The dial's printed face is checked by the heights of the parts over it.
  • Would it run? Whether a chronometer made to the model would run and keep time is worked out in two reports beside this page, each check with its method, its results and what they mean: Would a chronometer made to the model run and keep time? (the gears, temperature, the plates by finite elements, the hairspring, the detent under shock, the gimbals in a seaway, the mainspring's friction, every mechanism, the bench's tests and the rating trial) and Does the model behave like a real Hamilton Model 21? (its escapement against the sound and the swing of real ones on video).

Estimated

  • The centre pinion’s 14 leaves and the fusee arbor’s pinion of 12, inferred from the manual’s 56 hours on 17½ half turns and a photographed dial’s up–down scale; the other counts were made on a restoration video of a 1941 Model 21, and the 16-tooth escape wheel is Hamilton’s.
  • The fusee profile and its stop-bar’s size, the heights of the parts the side photograph doesn’t show (the third wheel, the barrel, the balance), and the mainspring’s coils.
  • The detent’s dimensions (its plan follows the manual’s Fig. 90, its settings the manual’s adjustment figures), the balance rim diameter, and the sustaining pawl’s position.
  • Above real time, the balance is shown swinging at a viewable rate.

Finish

Nickel plates with bold damascening and gilt wheels, as on the photographed movements. The gilt option matches the gilt movement in one of the reference photographs.

The Roman dial follows the A. Lange & Söhne (Glashütte) deck chronometers, with gilt hands and the maker’s name and number left off. Its wind scale is drawn over this movement’s 314° sweep rather than the Lange dial’s near-full circle, so the hand still reads true.

The Swiss dial follows the Ulysse Nardin (Le Locle) deck chronometers, such as the one the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey used in the 1940s (NOAA Heritage). The Soviet dial follows the First Moscow Watch Factory’s deck chronometers, which copied the Nardin layout with Arabic hours. Both leave off the maker’s name and number, and both draw their wind scales over this movement’s 314° sweep.
