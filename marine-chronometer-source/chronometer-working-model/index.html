Rate and timing weights

There is no regulator. The rate is set with the weights on the balance rim, each a nut on a screw: the two timing weights, or the small vernier weights for fine changes. Both of a pair are turned alike, so the balance stays in poise. Out moves their mass further from the staff, which raises the balance's moment of inertia and slows it: the hands lose.

The manual gives about 40 s a day for a full turn of the timing weights and 2.8 s for the verniers; the model's thread is set to match, and the moment of inertia comes from the model's balance and the parts list's masses. In an overhaul, balance screws and washers bring the rate within 2 minutes a day, then the timing weights within half a second, and the verniers make the final changes (Ops. 5, 6, 10). The model's clock runs at the new rate, so the hands drift from real time; try 3600×. The weights are drawn on the Hamilton balance only.

Rate book

The navigator doesn't set the hands: the chronometer's error is compared with a time signal, daily, and recorded with its rate, and applied to every reading (Sec. III, Setting; Sec. IX, Table I). Error and daily rate in seconds: + fast or gaining, − slow or losing. Here the signal is the master time, which runs with the model; the hands step in half seconds, so the error is read to the nearest half second.

Stopping and starting

A detent chronometer does not start by itself. Once its balance swings too little to carry the discharge jewel past the trip spring and unlock the wheel, it stops, and it takes a quick twist of the box to set the balance going again (Sec. III).

Balance locking arm

Train-blocking screw

The arm was fitted from 1947 so a chronometer could be shipped with its balance held; the screw keeps the mainspring's power in while the balance and escapement are out for service. With the screw down the balance swings on, unlocking a wheel that cannot turn, and runs down in a minute or two; raise the screw before then and it carries on. Screw it down or lock the arm, then unlock and twist: the hands will have lost the time it stood.

Adjuster's bench

The escapement's settings, as the repairman adjusts them (Sec. VIII, Ops. 84–88, 97). Move one and the detent, jewels and roller are rebuilt with it, and the figures are measured against the manual's the way tools/escapement.js measures them. If a setting would stop the escapement working, the bench says why and keeps the last one that works.

About this model

Orel Magidish (assisted by Claude Opus 5.5) – September 2026
orelmag@gmail.com

A working 3D model of the Hamilton Model 21 marine chronometer: a two-day fusee chronometer with a spring detent escapement, made in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, for the U.S. Navy from 1941. Every part runs from one clock: the balance beats twice a second, the hands step in half-seconds, and the fusee and chain follow the hours since winding.

Using it

  • Drag to turn; Shift-drag, middle-drag or drag two fingers to pan; scroll or pinch to zoom. The ? button on the model lists every control, for a mouse and for touch.
  • Tap a part to see what it does and its Hamilton part number.
  • Right-click or long-press a part to fade, hide or isolate it; do the same on empty space to bring hidden or isolated parts back.
  • Space bar stops and restarts the movement; keys 1 to 9 pick the views in the order of their buttons. With the model focused (click it, or Tab to it), the arrow keys turn the view, + and − zoom and 0 resets it. With reduced motion set in the system, views change without animation and the walkthrough leaves ship motion off.
  • Under Parts, single out or hide any part by name, including parts too small to tap.
  • The model keeps Greenwich Mean Time, as Navy chronometers were kept; under Time, Keep: Local time switches to your time zone. (The manual has the hands set only when the chronometer is started, the error being recorded instead; here Set the hands works at any time.)
  • Wind with the key (under Winding) turns the key half a turn at a time until the winding stop catches. Under Rate and timing weights, move the balance’s weights and watch the hands gain or lose.
  • It doesn’t start by itself. Run down, or stopped with the balance locking arm or the train-blocking screw (under Stopping and starting), it needs winding and then Twist to start, the quick twist of the box the manual prescribes; the hands lose the time it stood.

How it was made

  • Measured. The balance, fusee and barrel positions were triangulated from two photographs, each with its own fitted camera. The bridge outline, balance cock, setup cover, screws, engraving and damascene direction were traced on the top view; rendered from that photograph’s camera, the model’s key axes land within 0.4 mm of it. The heights of the plates, wheels and bridges come from a side photograph scaled by the pillar plate’s 3.86 mm edge.
  • Solved. The escape wheel sits 9.40 mm from the balance, where the manual’s impulse roller leaves its specified 0.002 in of roller shake. The third wheel’s position and the train’s tooth sizes were solved so that every arbor clears every wheel and the barrel.
  • Checked against the parts list. Every part in the manual’s parts lists (Figs. 106–110) was checked against the model, and the missing ones added: among them the pillar, mounting-ring and dial screws, the endstone caps, the balance’s hub cap and hold-down screws, the balance locking arm and the train-blocking screw. Every screw has its thread, and every part it passes through or screws into has its hole.
  • Checked. The parts were tested for collisions to 0.05 mm through a full escapement cycle, at many positions of the train and from run down to full wind, including the chain, the springs and the barrel, whose clearances were measured as it turns. Every screw, pivot and pawl was checked for proper seating and engagement. Not tested: the dial face. The split-balance variant is not yet clear of the barrel bridge.

Estimated

  • The tooth counts of the centre, third and fourth wheels and pinions, chosen to give the ratios a half-second train needs. The 16-tooth escape wheel and the 96:14 first stage (seven half turns of the key a day) are Hamilton’s.
  • The fusee profile and its stop-bar’s size, the heights of the parts the side photograph doesn’t show (the third wheel, the barrel, the balance), and the mainspring’s coils.
  • The detent’s dimensions (its plan follows the manual’s Fig. 90, its settings the manual’s adjustment figures), the balance rim diameter, and the sustaining pawl’s position.
  • Above real time, the balance is shown swinging at a viewable rate.

Finish

Nickel plates with bold damascening and gilt wheels, as on the photographed movements. The gilt option matches the gilt movement in one of the reference photographs.

The Roman dial follows the A. Lange & Söhne (Glashütte) deck chronometers, with gilt hands and the maker’s name and number left off. Its wind scale is drawn over this movement’s 240° sweep rather than the Lange dial’s near-full circle, so the hand still reads true.

The Swiss dial follows the Ulysse Nardin (Le Locle) deck chronometers, such as the one the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey used in the 1940s (NOAA Heritage). The Soviet dial follows the First Moscow Watch Factory’s deck chronometers, which copied the Nardin layout with Arabic hours. Both leave off the maker’s name and number, and both draw their wind scales over this movement’s 240° sweep.
