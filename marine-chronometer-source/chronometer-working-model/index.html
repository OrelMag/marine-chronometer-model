Rate and timing weights

There is no regulator. The rate is set with the weights on the balance rim, each a nut on a screw: the two timing weights, or the small vernier weights for fine changes. Both of a pair are turned alike, so the balance stays in poise. Out moves their mass further from the staff, which raises the balance's moment of inertia and slows it: the hands lose.

The manual gives about 40 s a day for a full turn of the timing weights and 2.8 s for the verniers; the model's thread is set to match, and the moment of inertia comes from the model's balance and the parts list's masses. In an overhaul, balance screws and washers bring the rate within 2 minutes a day, then the timing weights within half a second, and the verniers make the final changes (Ops. 5, 6, 10). The model's clock runs at the new rate, so the hands drift from real time; try 3600×. The screw pairs (numbered round from the arm) take the five head heights of Table II, and washers the six thicknesses of Table III, under both heads of a pair; the panel gives the manual's figure for the change beside the model's. Zero puts back the weights' mid-travel and the standard screws. The escapement's settings on the Adjuster's bench change the rate too, and the panel says by how much; the bench's own button takes them back. The weights and screws are drawn on the Hamilton balance only.

Rate book

The navigator doesn't set the hands: the chronometer's error is compared with a time signal, daily, and recorded with its rate, and applied to every reading (Sec. III, Setting; Sec. IX, Table I). Error and daily rate in seconds: + fast or gaining, − slow or losing. Here the signal is the master time, which runs with the model; the hands step in half seconds, so the error is read to the nearest half second.

Stopping and starting

A detent chronometer does not start by itself. Once its balance swings too little to carry the discharge jewel past the trip spring and unlock the wheel, it stops, and it takes a quick twist of the box to set the balance going again (Sec. III).

Balance locking arm

Train-blocking screw

The arm was fitted from 1947 so a chronometer could be shipped with its balance held; the screw keeps the mainspring's power in while the balance and escapement are out for service. With the screw down the balance swings on, unlocking a wheel that cannot turn, and runs down in a minute or two; raise the screw before then and it carries on. Screw it down or lock the arm, then unlock and twist: the hands will have lost the time it stood.

Adjuster's bench

The escapement's settings, as the repairman adjusts them (Sec. VIII, Ops. 84–88, 97). Move one and the detent, jewels and roller are rebuilt with it, and the figures are measured against the manual's the way tools/escapement.js measures them. The model runs with them: the balance swings as far as the impulse, less what unlocking takes, carries it (Stopping and starting), and the escapement's rate changes with where they push the balance about its dead point (Rate and timing weights). If a setting would stop the escapement working, or leave the balance swinging too little to keep it going, the bench says why and keeps the last one that works.

How a brass bowl hung in a mahogany box told a navigator where on Earth the ship was. This essay takes the instrument apart one idea at a time, using the Hamilton Model 21 on the other tab: every figure is drawn from that model’s own parts, counts and settings.

Time is a position

Latitude was never the hard problem at sea. The height of the noon sun, or of the pole star, above the horizon tells a navigator how far north or south the ship is, and a sextant measures that angle well.

Longitude is different, because the Earth turns under the sky: 360° every 24 hours, 15° every hour. An east–west position is really a question about time. When the sun stands highest over the ship it is local noon, and if the navigator knows what time it is at that moment at a reference meridian such as Greenwich, the difference between the two times is the ship’s longitude.

Move the ship with the slider. The view looks down on the North Pole with the sun to the right, always at the moment of the ship’s local noon:

Local noon is easy to observe. Greenwich time in the middle of an ocean is not: it has to be carried aboard, in a clock that keeps going through months of motion, damp and heat.

And the arithmetic is unforgiving. Four minutes of time are a degree of longitude, and four seconds are a minute of arc, which is a nautical mile at the equator and less toward the poles, where the meridians crowd together. A clock that gains two seconds a day is superb by the standards of a house; after six weeks at sea it has moved the ship twenty miles.

John Harrison’s fourth timekeeper, H4, beat that standard on its 1761–62 trial voyage to Jamaica. But H4 was a single, intricate masterpiece that few could copy. The instrument that went to sea by the thousand in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries came from a different line, through Pierre Le Roy in France and John Arnold and Thomas Earnshaw in London: a mainspring and fusee, a spring detent escapement, a compensated balance with a helical spring, hung in gimbals in a wooden box. The Hamilton Model 21, made in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, for the U.S. Navy from 1941, is one of the last of that line, and it is the one this essay takes apart.

An oscillator that ignores the sea

Every clock is an oscillator and a counter. On land the best oscillator was the pendulum, but a pendulum keeps time against gravity, and a rolling ship adds accelerations of the same order as gravity itself. A pendulum clock on a deck is hopeless.

The chronometer uses a balance: a wheel that turns back and forth on its own axis, drawn back toward its rest position by a spring. Because the wheel is balanced about its axis, a sideways jolt pushes equally on both sides of it and makes no turning force. Only a turning of the ship itself can disturb it, and the gimbals we’ll meet later take care of most of that.

Its period depends on two things: its moment of inertia I, which grows with the rim’s mass and with how far out that mass sits, and the stiffness κ of the spring. For small swings the period is T = 2π√(I/κ). A heavier rim swings more slowly; a stiffer spring swings faster.

The Model 21’s balance makes one full oscillation in half a second. Watchmakers count each swing in one direction as a vibration, so that is 14,400 vibrations an hour, and the escapement pushes the balance once in every oscillation, which is why the hands step in half-seconds. By the model’s arithmetic the balance, with its screws and weights, has a moment of inertia of about 930 g·mm², so its hairspring has to turn it back with about 147 micronewton-metres for each radian of twist.

Hamilton’s rim is solid and uncut. It is free of the centrifugal effects that open a split rim at speed, so it can swing further, 1⅜ to 1½ turns from one end of its swing to the other (the model swings it 1.42), and it could be made larger and 35 percent lighter for the same moment of inertia, which eases its pivots.

The spring that breathes

An ideal oscillator is isochronous: its period doesn’t depend on how widely it swings. A real balance only approximates that, and it matters, because the swing never stays put. It changes as the mainspring runs down, as the oil thickens, as warmth changes the friction. Any link between the swing and the period turns each of those into an error of rate.

Most watches use a flat spiral balance spring. Chronometers usually use a helical one, a coil shaped like a small cylinder, with its ends bent inward in terminal curves so that they attach close to the axis. With well-shaped curves the coil stays centred on the balance staff as it winds and unwinds, so it only ever twists the balance. Without them the coil’s centre wanders to one side as it winds, pushing the pivots sideways and adding friction that depends on the swing.

Arnold was curving the ends of helical springs by the 1770s and 1780s, largely by experiment; a full mathematical treatment came from the French engineer Édouard Phillips in the 1860s.

Hamilton’s spring is of Hamilton Elinvar, an alloy chosen for elasticity that stays steady with temperature, and it doesn’t corrode. Each end is held in its clamp by a wedge pin, without bending the spring, so its active length is the same winding and unwinding, which the manual credits with reducing its isochronal error. There is no regulator to lengthen or shorten it: the rate is set with the balance’s screws and weights alone.

The Model 21’s balance close up, with its timing weights

Heat

Temperature was the chronometer’s chief enemy. As a steel spring warms it weakens, and at the same time the balance expands, moving its mass further from the axis. Both slow the clock. A plain brass balance with a steel spring loses on the order of ten seconds a day for every degree Celsius, and a ship sailing from the English Channel to the tropics sees twenty degrees or more.

The classic cure was a balance that changes shape. Its rim is a strip of two metals fused together, brass outside and steel inside, cut through near each end of the arm so each half has a free end. Brass expands more than steel, so as it warms the free ends curl inward, drawing mass toward the axis just enough to offset the weakening spring. Weights moved along the rim set how much correction each degree brings.

That correction isn’t perfect. The bimetallic rim changes the moment of inertia roughly in proportion to temperature, while the spring’s loss of stiffness has a large quadratic part. Adjusted to keep time at two temperatures, a chronometer gains between them and loses outside them. This middle temperature error, typically a second or two a day, occupied makers for most of the nineteenth century, until Charles-Édouard Guillaume’s nickel steels largely solved it around 1900.

The Model 21 is built on Guillaume’s alloys. Its rim is solid stainless steel, silver-soldered to an arm of Invar, which hardly expands. The rim’s diameter at the ends of the arm stays almost fixed; the rest of the rim moves out as it warms and in as it cools, so screws moved toward or away from the arm set the compensation, to within 0.02 seconds a day for each degree Fahrenheit. The Elinvar hairspring hardly changes its stiffness. Adjusted, its temperature error is negligible, the manual says, and in service no temperature record needs to be kept.

Constant force

The energy is stored in a coiled mainspring inside a drum, the barrel. A spring pulls harder fully wound than nearly run down, and more force swings the balance wider; since no balance is perfectly isochronous, the rate would follow the state of wind. The chronometer borrows a much older answer, the fusee: a grooved cone, joined to the barrel by a fine steel chain.

Fully wound, the chain pulls on the narrow end of the cone, where it has the least leverage, just when the spring is strongest. As the spring weakens, the chain comes off the cone at larger and larger radii, and the pull times the lever stays the same.

Unlike a watch’s, the barrel drives no wheel at all: the first wheel of the train is on the fusee. The barrel’s own arbor never turns in use; a setup ratchet and pawl on the barrel bridge hold it, and only a watchmaker’s let-down key moves it.

The fusee and chain in the working model

Winding

Winding turns all of this backward. The key turns the fusee arbor counterclockwise, drawing the chain off the barrel and onto the cone, which winds the spring. For as long as the key turns, though, the fusee isn’t pushing the train forward. Harrison’s maintaining power fills the gap: in the Model 21 a sustaining spring inside the fusee wheel, always under load, drives the train for five to ten minutes, and a sustaining pawl keeps it from unwinding backward.

The routine was the same every day, at the same hour. Turn the chronometer gently face down in its gimbals; turn the shield plate on the bottom of the case clockwise to uncover the keyhole; wind to the left. Seven half turns restore the 24 hours since the last winding. The last half turn goes slowly, so that the chain meets the winding stop gently: at full wind it pushes a stop-bar in the top of the fusee out against a stop under the barrel bridge, and the key can turn no further.

The dial keeps the account. Its up–down indicator is driven by a pinion on the fusee arbor, and after the daily winding its hand should have gone back from about 24 to 0. The Model 21 is designed to run at most 56 hours on a winding (the model’s chain, like the real one, is all on the barrel after 56). Allowed to run down, it needs 17½ half turns, and then, since its escapement can’t start itself, a single quick twist of the box to set the balance swinging.

Winding without stopping, in the working model

Counting

From the fusee, a train of wheels both slows things down and counts. The fusee wheel drives the centre wheel, which turns once an hour and carries the minute hand. Each wheel after it drives the next one faster: the third wheel, then the fourth, which turns once a minute and carries the second hand, and finally the escape wheel.

The escape wheel releases one tooth per oscillation of the balance, 120 times a minute. Hamilton gave it 16 teeth, where most chronometers have 13 or 15, so it turns once every 8 seconds. At the other end the fusee wheel’s 90 teeth drive the centre wheel’s pinion of 14, so the fusee turns once in 6.43 hours, and the manual’s 17½ half turns of the key wind the 56 hours it gives as the Model 21’s longest run. Hamilton’s counts for the wheels between aren’t published; the model’s were counted on a restoration video of a 1941 Model 21, all but the centre pinion’s, whose 14 leaves are inferred from those 56 hours.

At normal speed the motion isn’t smooth: the whole train stands still, then jumps, twice a second. That is the escapement at work, and in a chronometer it is the most distinctive part of all.

The Model 21’s going train in the working model

The detent

An escapement has two jobs: to let the train advance one tooth at a time, and to give the balance a small push to replace what friction takes. Every contact disturbs the balance a little, so the ideal escapement touches it as rarely and as briefly as it can. The spring detent comes very close. It pushes the balance in one direction of swing only, and for the rest of each oscillation the balance swings almost free.

Here is one full cycle, seen from the balance cock’s side as the manual’s Fig. 90 draws it. The escape wheel, 13.16 mm across, is held by a jewel, the locking jewel, on the detent: a slender blade whose foot is screwed to a fixed block and joined to it by a thin spring, so that it can swing a little. A stop button sets how far it falls back. The balance staff carries two rollers, each with its jewel.

As the balance swings one way, the small unlocking jewel meets the tip of the fine trip spring, which rests against the detent’s horn, so the push lifts the whole detent off its stop. The locking jewel moves clear, the wheel leaps forward and a tooth drops into the notch cut in the larger roller, where it catches the impulse jewel and drives the balance on its way. By then the detent has sprung back, ready to catch the next tooth.

On the return swing the unlocking jewel meets the trip spring from the other side. This time the trip spring simply bends away from the horn: the detent never moves, the wheel stays locked, and the balance passes almost without resistance.

All of this happens within a few degrees of a swing of 255° each way, and the settings are fine. The manual gives the repairer figures to set, measured as angles of the balance: the lock, from the moment the detent leaves its stop until the tooth drops off the locking jewel, about 6°; the let-off, at least 6°; the wheel’s drop onto the impulse jewel, about 2°; and the overall, the whole contact of both swings together, 26° to 30°. The impulse roller, 6.3 mm across, clears the teeth by about 0.05 mm, and the horn clears the unlocking jewel by a quarter of a millimetre. As the model sets it: lock 6.0°, let-off 10.6°, drop 2.1°, overall 28.4°, roller shake 0.055 mm, horn clearance 0.25 mm.

Hamilton chose the materials for the job: the detent is beryllium copper, springy and rustless, and the trip spring is Elinvar. In English chronometers it was usually gold.

The design has a price. A detent escapement doesn’t start itself: once the balance swings less than about 41° each way, it can no longer unlock the wheel and see the impulse through, and the chronometer stops until it is given a twist. A hard jolt can also knock the detent and let the wheel slip a tooth. In a padded box in a chart room that is an acceptable trade, and it is one reason the design stayed in the box instead of moving to the wrist.

Level on a moving ship

A balance’s rate varies slightly with its position, so a chronometer is adjusted to run in one: dial up, balance level. The brass case hangs in gimbals, a ring pivoted in the box on one axis, with the case pivoted in the ring on the axis at right angles. Weighted low, the case stays level while the box rolls and pitches around it.

A chronometer went to and from a ship in its box, carried by hand. The gimbal latch locks the case to the ring and the ring to the box, for carrying and before the hands are set.

Keeping the rate

A navigator never set the hands. They were set only when the chronometer was started; after that its error was recorded and allowed for. Every day, or at other convenient times, the dial was compared with a master time signal, an authorized radio signal, either directly or through a comparing watch, which adds a small error of its own. The hands step in half-seconds, so the error is read to the nearest half-second.

The daily rate is the change in that error from one day to the next. Read to the half-second, single days are noisy, so the rates are averaged over several days into a mean daily rate, and that is what works out the true time between signals. Under Remarks goes anything that might have disturbed it: started, stopped, set, moved.

What matters, then, isn’t that a chronometer keeps perfect time but that its rate is steady. One that gains 1.3 seconds a day, every day, is as good as a perfect one; one whose rate wanders is not. That is why each capital ship of the Navy, battleship, carrier or cruiser, was allotted two or more Model 21s, kept in different stations aboard: the navigator compares them, and a misbehaving instrument gives itself away.

Before issue, each chronometer was tested for thirty days in a dust-free room held at 72½ °F, compared every day on Hamilton’s electronic comparator, which reads the dial to better than 0.02 seconds. The thirty days made six periods of five, at 90, 72½, 55, 55, 72½ and 90 °F. From each period’s mean rate came the figures the Bureau of Ships held it to: no period’s mean rate over 1.55 seconds a day; the rates at 55 and 90 °F within 1.2 seconds a day of each other; and the two periods at the same temperature within 0.7 seconds a day.

The Hamilton Model 21

The Model 21 was designed and made by the Hamilton Watch Company of Lancaster, Pennsylvania, to U.S. Navy Specification 18-C-7; the dial in these pages is from one made under a U.S. Maritime Commission contract. The Navy assigned its serial numbers and engraved them on the dial and the barrel bridge. It has fourteen jewels. It changed in service: chronometers put into the field before 1944 may have a one-piece roller, replaced at overhaul by two, whose angle can be set; later ones have a train-blocking screw, from serial 4003, to hold the mainspring’s power while the balance and escapement are out; and from the end of 1947 a balance locking arm was fitted at overhaul, in place of the folded wedges that had held the balance still in shipment.

Most of what is known of it in this detail comes from one book, the Navy’s Manual for Overhaul, Repair and Handling of Hamilton Ship Chronometer (NAVSHIPS 250-624, 1948). The model on the other tab is built from it and from photographs of surviving movements; the Royal Museums Greenwich holds one, No. 4E019.

Radio time signals, and later satellite navigation, retired the chronometer from its work. The instrument itself, a small steel wheel swinging twice a second to keep a ship’s place on the planet, remains one of the most elegant answers ever built to a problem that once cost thousands of lives.

See it working: every part can be picked out and named, and the hands keep Greenwich time, as the Navy’s chronometers were kept.

A working 3D model of the Hamilton Model 21 marine chronometer: a two-day fusee chronometer with a spring detent escapement, made in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, for the U.S. Navy from 1941. Every part runs from one clock: the balance beats twice a second, the hands step in half-seconds, and the fusee and chain follow the hours since winding.

Using it

  • Drag to turn; Shift-drag, middle-drag or drag two fingers to pan; scroll or pinch to zoom. The ? button on the model lists every control, for a mouse and for touch.
  • Tap a part to see what it does and its Hamilton part number.
  • Right-click or long-press a part to fade, hide or isolate it; do the same on empty space to bring hidden or isolated parts back.
  • Space bar stops and restarts the movement; keys 1 to 9 pick the views in the order of their buttons. With the model focused (click it, or Tab to it), the arrow keys turn the view, + and − zoom and 0 resets it. With reduced motion set in the system, views change without animation and the walkthrough leaves ship motion off.
  • Under Parts, single out or hide any part by name, including parts too small to tap.
  • The model keeps Greenwich Mean Time, as Navy chronometers were kept; under Time, Keep: Local time switches to your time zone. (The manual has the hands set only when the chronometer is started, the error being recorded instead; here Set the hands works at any time.)
  • Wind with the key (under Winding) turns the key half a turn at a time until the winding stop catches. Under Rate and timing weights, move the balance’s weights and watch the hands gain or lose.
  • It doesn’t start by itself. Run down, or stopped with the balance locking arm or the train-blocking screw (under Stopping and starting), it needs winding and then Twist to start, the quick twist of the box the manual prescribes; the hands lose the time it stood.

How it was made

  • Measured. The balance, fusee and barrel positions were triangulated from two photographs, each with its own fitted camera. The bridge outline, balance cock, setup cover, screws, engraving and damascene direction were traced on the top view; rendered from that photograph’s camera, the model’s key axes land within 0.4 mm of it. The heights of the plates, wheels and bridges come from a side photograph scaled by the pillar plate’s 3.86 mm edge.
  • Solved. The escape wheel sits 9.40 mm from the balance, where the manual’s impulse roller leaves its specified 0.002 in of roller shake. The third wheel’s position and the train’s tooth sizes were solved so that every arbor clears every wheel and the barrel.
  • Checked against the parts list. Every part in the manual’s parts lists (Figs. 106–110) was checked against the model, and the missing ones added: among them the pillar, mounting-ring and dial screws, the endstone caps, the balance’s hub cap and hold-down screws, the balance locking arm and the train-blocking screw. Every screw has its thread, and every part it passes through or screws into has its hole.
  • Checked. The parts were tested for collisions to 0.05 mm through a full escapement cycle, at many positions of the train and from run down to full wind, including the chain, the springs and the barrel, whose clearances were measured as it turns. Every screw, pivot and pawl was checked for proper seating and engagement. The dial's printed face is checked by the heights of the parts over it.

Finish

Nickel plates with bold damascening and gilt wheels, as on the photographed movements. The gilt option matches the gilt movement in one of the reference photographs.

The Roman dial follows the A. Lange & Söhne (Glashütte) deck chronometers, with gilt hands and the maker’s name and number left off. Its wind scale is drawn over this movement’s 314° sweep rather than the Lange dial’s near-full circle, so the hand still reads true.

The Swiss dial follows the Ulysse Nardin (Le Locle) deck chronometers, such as the one the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey used in the 1940s (NOAA Heritage). The Soviet dial follows the First Moscow Watch Factory’s deck chronometers, which copied the Nardin layout with Arabic hours. Both leave off the maker’s name and number, and both draw their wind scales over this movement’s 314° sweep.
