Marine Chronometer model · verification for a build · · with it: the model against real chronometers

Would a chronometer made to the model run and keep time?

The aim has changed from parity with the 1948 Hamilton Model 21 to a working marine chronometer, made with a CNC and a 3D printer, steady enough to navigate by. The model already agreed with the manual and with recordings of real Model 21s; this report covers the checks added to answer the maker's question: will the gears run when the holes are bored by a machine, will it hold together and stay in adjustment from cold to hot, does the hairspring do what its design says, and how will the made chronometer be tested and rated. Every figure here is computed by a script in the repository, named beside it, from the model's own geometry and numbers.

Approach

A chronometer keeps time if three things hold: its train delivers a steady torque to the escapement (so the balance's swing, and with it the rate, stays put), its geometry stays in adjustment under load and temperature, and its balance and hairspring oscillate at a rate that changes little with swing and temperature. The model's existing checks cover the escapement in detail (its figures against the manual, its motion in time, the energy budget, the stresses and an error budget for a voyage). Nine checks were added for the build:

Units: millimetres and micrometres (µm), newtons, seconds a day (s/d, + gaining). The swing is the balance's amplitude, each way; the manual asks for 1⅜–1½ turns, 247.5–270°, and the model runs at 255°. A rate of 4 s of time is a mile of longitude at the equator.

1. The gears, tooth by tooth

The going train is four meshes: the fusee wheel drives the centre pinion, then centre → third → fourth → escape pinion. The model draws its teeth to BS 978 Part 2's clock proportions (cycloidal wheel teeth: an epicycloidal addendum, radial flanks; pinion leaves with round tips), which the manual doesn't give and the model estimates. gearing.js takes those outlines from the model's own code (core.js gearOutline), sampled finer (400 points an addendum, 240 a tip), and places wheel and pinion at their centre distance m(z₁+z₂)/2. For each of 120 steps through one pitch of the wheel it turns the pinion back against its load until the outlines touch (bisection to well under a micrometre), takes the contact's normal from the touching outline and the slip from the two bodies' velocities, and so the torque the pinion receives against the wheel's, with tooth friction µ (0.10–0.20: hardened steel on brass, unlubricated, the range physics.js uses). A ratio of 1 is a perfect, frictionless mesh.

None of the four meshes drops (the next tooth always engages before the last lets go), about a fifth of the action is before the line of centres, and the mean loss is 2–5 % a mesh. The escape wheel's torque is the product of the four ratios as their wheels turn (the centre once an hour, the fusee wheel once in 6.4 h); the balance's swing follows it through the escapement's own equation (shared/escapement.js: A² relaxes towards ampAt(drive)² with the balance's free run-down time TF = 67 s), and its rate through rateAt.

The ripple moves the rate by a few hundredths of a second a day for minutes at a time and repeats every day, so a navigator's daily rate takes it in; the time it wanders about its mean is . What matters is that the swing never leaves the band.

The depthing window and the CNC's tolerance

Each mesh's centres were then moved, the others left as drawn, until one of three things happened: it jams (the wheel's tips reach the pinion's root, or no backlash is left), its extra friction takes the day's mean swing out of the band with the set-up as drawn, or, the set-up restoring the mean (Op. 13), its ripple takes the swing out at some moment.

Each arbor has two holes, each off by up to t in any direction, and its pivots shake about 10 µm in their holes (estimated: 1–2 % of a jewelled pivot's diameter), so a mesh's centres close by at most 2t + 10 µm. Aimed at the drawn centres (BS 978's depth, kept for the teeth's sake: wider would leave less engagement for wear and knocks), the tightest mesh sets t = ± µm: a good CNC holds it with the holes bored, not drilled, and checked with gauge pins. Every mesh takes several times as much error wide as close, so a hole that errs should err wide.

2. From 40 to 100 °F

The movement is brass where it holds things (plates, bridges, pillars, wheels) and steel where it turns or locks (pinions, arbors, escape wheel, roller, detent). Brass grows × 10⁻⁶ a °C more than hardened steel (published ranges: leaded clock brass 18.7–20.9, carbon steel 10.8–12.5). From 20 °C, where the movement is put together, to 40 °F and 100 °F (wider than the Navy test's 55–90 °F, for a cabin at sea):

A mesh's depth changes only by the pinion's radius times the difference, because the wheel and its hole are both brass. The endshake, over the train's 16.8 mm span (measured on a side photograph), changes by : set at µm at 20 °C, it stays inside Ops. 15, 69 and 74's 0.001–0.003 in at both ends. In the escapement, the balance's and the detent foot's places are brass-held and the wheel, roller and detent are steel; makeEsc rebuilt with those distances grown moves lock and drop a tenth of a degree and the rate a few hundredths of a second a day. The temperature error is the balance's and its spring's alone, which is measured, not computed (section 5).

3. Finite elements on the plates and bridges

The largest loads in the movement are the fusee chain's pull (the mainspring's moment on the barrel's pitch radius, physics.js) and the first mesh's tooth force. fea.py takes the six parts that hold pivots from the page itself (each part's solid, closed and consistently wound, solids.py): the pillar plate, the train, barrel, escape and lower bridges, and the bridge on the dial side carrying the third's and fourth's lower jewels. A tetrahedral mesher (gmsh) was tried first; it found self-overlapping facets in the page's surfaces, so each solid is instead cut into voxel bricks (a ray up each column finding where it is inside), 0.25–0.55 mm in plan and 6–14 layers through the thickness. Trilinear bricks, brass at E = 100 GPa (97–110), ν = 0.34, assembled in numpy and solved by conjugate gradients preconditioned with smoothed-aggregation multigrid (pyamg), the six rigid-body modes given.

Loads, every stage lossless (the largest): the chain's pull along the external tangent of the fusee and barrel at its height on them (fully wound on the fusee's top turn, run down on its bottom), and at each mesh the tooth force, tangential from the torque and radial from the mesh's mean pressure angle (section 1, 9–11°). Each is split between its arbor's two bearings by the lever rule; a pivot's load goes on its hole's wall over the half it bears on, as cos θ. Supports: a bridge is held where it is screwed (every node within the screw head's radius, through its thickness); the pillar plate at its rim, where the case's seat ring takes it. Assembly by superposition: each fixing's reaction is put on what is beneath it (another part, or a pillar, a 6.7 mm brass rod fixed at both ends, bending as a beam); each part then follows its fixings as a rigid body fitted in plan, plus its own bending. The run is repeated with coarser bricks as a convergence check.

The plates and bridges are many times stiffer than they need to be: the hole positions and the depthing are what the maker must hold, not the parts' stiffness.

4. The hairspring, by a second method

The model's hairspring is designed, not drawn by eye (shared/hairspring.js): a helix of 12 turns, r 5.1 mm, with terminal curves solved to Phillips' conditions (1861) so its pull on the balance is a pure couple, and checked at large swings as an inextensible planar elastica solved by shooting (hairspring.js). hairspring-fe.js solves the same path a different way: co-rotational beam elements (Crisfield 1991, ch. 7; each element straight between its nodes, axial and bending stiffness, the tangent consistent), the strip extensible (0.221 mm thick: EA/EI = 12/t²), the stud's end clamped and the collet's end turned about the staff in 2.5° steps to ±300°, each step to equilibrium by Newton's method with a block-tridiagonal factorization.

The two agree on the couple's shape to a few parts in 10⁷ and on the force to two figures; their absolute stiffnesses differ by 5 × 10⁻⁴, the elements' chords being shorter than the curve. The spring's own isochronism, the rate it gives across the manual's band, is . What neither holds: the strip's own mass, and the elastic modulus's change with temperature, which is the spring maker's to set and the bench's to measure.

5. Tests on the bench

The analysis says the design works; only tests on the made parts say a chronometer made to it does. bench.js turns the model's figures into tests with pass figures; makers.py puts each on its part's card (its maker's sheet) and the whole list in the build book's last chapter (Making it → Build book). benchcheck.py judges the maker's measurements against the same figures (a JSON file it makes to fill in) and says what to do where one fails: the set-up turns that bring the mainspring into its band or even the fusee (from the curve's slope and the fusee's 1.96:1 profile), the timing weights or the millimetres to take at the stud, the manual's operation that sets an escapement figure. Its --demo plants eight faults in a made-up set and finds all eight.

6. The rating trial

The made chronometer is rated as the Navy rated the Model 21 (Sec. IX: thirty days in six periods of five, at 90, 72½, 55, 55, 72½ and 90 °F, compared daily with a time signal). rating.py reads the maker's log (the time, the chronometer's error, the temperature) and works the test's figures exactly as the model's test card does (app.js testCalc), fits the daily rates as a constant, a linear drift and a temperature curve with the rest as day-to-day scatter, backtests a ten-day rate carried on through the log itself, and projects 2000 voyages. Below it is run on a made-up log with known properties (the error budget's chronometer like No. 3390: rate +0.35 s/d, scatter 0.042 s/d, a temperature curve), sixty days, the first thirty the Navy's periods.

The fit recovers what the log was made with (the rate to 0.01 s/d, the scatter to 0.002), so on a real log its figures can be trusted as far as the log goes. Good enough to navigate by is taken as 95 % of projected voyages under 30 s (7½ miles) after 30 days.

7. The build's deviations from the Model 21

The checks above found nothing that needs changing for the chronometer to work; two changes, both with 1950s materials, make it easier to make and steadier to keep. Each is on its part's card in the page as the build's, with the Model 21's original beside it and a button back to it (the link keeps orig= for one set back); the 3D model stays the Model 21. tools/deviations.js works their numbers and writes deviations.json, which makers.py puts on the sheets.

The balance's temperature compensation, from Table IV

The build keeps the Model 21's own balance, a stainless rim on an Invar arm, and takes only the hairspring in Nivarox. Until 13 October 2026 it took a one-piece Glucydur balance too; that has nothing to trim, so the spring's thermoelastic coefficient would have had to be held within ± × 10⁻⁶ a °C to meet the Navy's 1.20 s a day between 55 and 90 °F, about a seventh of the ±10 a bought spring holds. On the Model 21's balance, Table IV's screw moves (Op. 8) trim what the spring misses.

Method. The rim grows as stainless, while the arm's ends hold it to Invar's growth, so the rim is pulled in at the arm and bulges at the quarter, as a thin ring under two opposite forces does: its radial deflection goes as Σ cos nθ / (n² − 1)², n = 2, 4, 6 … (the ring's bending by Fourier series, which gives Roark's 0.1366 and 0.1488 P R³ / E I for its two diameters). A screw pair of mass M at radius r moved from the quarter (hole 7) to hole n then changes the rate in heat by M r C (S(θn) − S(θ7)) ΔT / I of a day. The one unknown, C, is fitted to all forty of Table IV's figures (holes 8–12 under eight heads), each pair's mass from the parts list. Then the whole balance's expansion (rim, arm, hub, the standard screws and the weights) gives the coefficient the spring needs, and every placing of the four standard pairs in holes 3–12 gives what Table IV can trim.

Figures.

Estimated: Nivarox's expansion (7–10 × 10⁻⁶/°C) and modulus (165–195 GPa), as Elinvar-type alloys'; Invar 1.0–1.6 × 10⁻⁶/°C; the ring taken as inextensible and the arm as rigid; the 0.120 in head's mass extrapolated from the parts list's; I_REST (the 8 % of the moment the drawing lacks) anywhere between Invar's growth and the rim's. The temperature test on the bench (the rate, the last of section 5's tests) settles the pair and says which pairs to move.

8. The detent under shock: does a tooth ever get past?

The classic way a detent escapement fails at sea is tripping: the escape wheel lets a tooth go without the balance having unlocked it, and the chronometer gains a beat. The detent is the weak point: a thin blade on a thinner spring, held on its stop by a preload of a few millinewtons and nothing else, with the locking stone at its end. dynamics.js takes it as one rigid body turning about the root of its spring; that answers whether it is home in time, but not whether the blade, still ringing from its landing or from the tooth's lock, lifts the stone off the next tooth, nor how hard a knock must be once its own bending is counted. tripping.js takes the detent as what it is.

Method. The detent becomes a plane frame (): beam elements with stretching, bending and their consistent mass for the two-strip spring, the blade, the arm out to the horn and the trip spring, and rigid bodies for the cross-piece with its bracket, the jewel block with the stone, and the horn; every size is the drawn part's (movement.js, ESC.pieces), every material dynamics.js's (beryllium copper 128 GPa, Elinvar-type 180 GPa). Its modes, clamped at the foot, carry it in time with the balance and the escape wheel as dynamics.js does, but every contact is elastic: the discharge jewel's own outline on the trip spring's tip (pushing it onto the horn on the active swing, bending it away on the return), the trip spring on the horn, the blade on its stop button, the tooth on the locking stone (the stone's outline and draw from makeEsc, so the tooth is pushed back as the stone lifts) and on the impulse jewel. A contact is a stiff spring with a dashpot set for its restitution (penalty contact). Op. 78 sets the preload: the spring is bent so a 0.770 g weight on the locking jewel parts the blade from its button by approximately 0.005 in (p. 61), which leaves it 0.63 g of preload at the jewel as drawn and none for a spring 0.137 mm thick.

Validated. Made rigid and hinged at the spring's root, with the draw counted as dynamics.js counts it, the frame is dynamics.js's detent (stiffness, preload and first mode to 0.1 %) and runs as dynamics.js does: . Two things the frame finds that the rigid model leaves out:

  • The spring turns about its middle, not its root. A 3.8 mm spring under an 11 mm lever bends along its length, so the detent is stiffer at the stone than dynamics.js's hinge, and its first mode is Hz rather than . Running, that changes (the detent leaves the jewel and lands sooner): a constant the timing screws take up. The preload, which Op. 78 sets by weight, is the same either way.
  • The wheel recoils with its own inertia. dynamics.js holds the wheel still while the stone lifts and counts the draw as a steady moment. In the frame the jewel's strike starts the stone at once, the stone's 10° of draw throws the wheel back at 2–3 rad/s, and the tooth catches the stone up a few times before the release. Against the steady moment that changes : the averaged draw holds.
  • The tooth is free before makeEsc lets it go. makeEsc's recoil (the wheel's turn back as the stone lifts) peaks where the tooth's tip reaches the flat's inner end, the depth of lock, µm at the stone; past it the tip rides round the stone's rounded edge, where its pressure pushes the stone out, and it is free at µm. makeEsc keeps lifting to µm as room to spare and releases there. The frame lets the tooth go where it is free (), and measures every margin below against the depth of lock: a held tooth whose stone is lifted past it throws the stone off.

What the bench checks

Two things the frame shows a maker can measure. The lock (Op. 85): run at shallower locks (each in its own process, makeEsc's depth of lock changed, the frame as drawn and with every contact four times stiffer, 20 beats each), the stone's largest lift while it holds a tooth stays at whatever the depth, so a shallower lock leaves less margin: The blow that trips it hardly changes with the lock (the first table below): against knocks it is Op. 78's preload that holds the detent, which makes the weight test the one to do carefully. The detent spring, whose section no source gives: clamped by its foot and flicked, the detent rings at its first mode, , and the pitch rises nearly as its spring's thickness to the 1.5, so a phone's recording measures the spring (benchcheck.py --ring reads one). The largest lift over 20 beats is a maximum of a chaotic chatter: two runs a hair apart differ by about ten points. The figures are margins, not sharp limits; the lock's floor is where the margin falls below two, and the spring's band is the drawn spring to the thickest that stays under that margin and that Op. 78's test can still preload (0.137 mm, tolerances.js) ().

9. The box and gimbals in a seaway

A chronometer at sea hangs in gimbals so that the balance's staff stays upright whatever the ship does. The checks above took that as given. Here it is worked out: does the gimballed case keep the movement level, can it be set swinging by the ship or by the balance's own beat, and what reaches the escapement?

Method. The case with the movement in it and the gimbal ring are weighed on the model (massprops.py: every solid integrated at its material's density, for mass, centre of gravity and inertia about the gimbal centre), then run as two rigid bodies on their pivots, the ring on the box's at 3 and 9 and the case on the ring's at 6 and 12 (gimbals.js: Newton-Euler about the gimbal centre, which moves with the box; RK4). The ship's roll, pitch, yaw and heave are narrow-band random about each motion's period, the box some metres above the ship's centre of motion. Merchant ships roll at periods of 10-30 s (MARIN; Jachowski et al.); NORDFORSK's operability limit puts the box's athwartships acceleration at 0.12 g rms at the bridge, and one sea is set to it. The other levels, a small vessel's motion and the box's place in the ship are estimated. What the escapement feels, the force in its plane and the turn about the staff, is run through dynamics.js for 100 s against still water.

  • Level it. As drawn the case's pivots aren't over its centre of gravity, so it would hang out of level. The manual levels a chronometer when it first goes into its gimbals "by adjusting the position of the case brackets and gimbal support straps" (p. 13); the made one must be levelled the same way, and the analysis above assumes it is.
  • What it leaves out. The box's inside, which sets where the case meets it (), is estimated, as are the pivots' damping, the blows' stiffness and restitution, and the box's place in the ship.

10. The mainspring's coil friction

A mainspring takes more torque to wind than it gives back running: its coils slide on each other as it winds and unwinds, and rub. The energy budget had allowed for it as a published 5-15 %, the largest number in it taken on trust. Here it is worked out on the model's own spring.

Method. mainspring.py solves the strip's shape at every quarter turn of the wind as the one of least bending energy, its coils kept apart but frictionless (js/mainspring.js). Each of those states is polished by the same solve (mainspring_friction.py). The slip follows the steel: the two pieces that touch, on a coil and on the one over it, are followed from one state to the next, and the difference of their movements along the strip is how far they slide on each other; on the barrel's wall against the barrel's turn, on the core against the arbor's. The pressure between packed coils comes from the packed spiral's ring equations: each coil a ring bent by its own moment, EI(k − k₀), the torque the same at every section, so each coil's hoop force is (τ − M)/r, and each ring's radial balance passes the pressure on to the next from the pack's free edge (the solver's own multipliers and the rod's equations by differentiation were tried first, and failed their own checks in the packs: the second derivatives of a sampled shape are too rough). The friction's work, μ times pressure times slip, over each quarter turn is a torque that adds to winding and comes off running. μ: greased steel on steel, 0.08-0.2 lubricated (the CRC Handbook, as cited in US 3,939,081); 0.10 taken, 0.08 and 0.16 tried.

The build's strip, in Nivaflex

Method. The build's mainspring (section 7) is Nivaflex 45/5, 0.386 mm thick for the model's torque (its modulus 220 GPa against carbon steel's 207), the same length, width and free curve (a spring set the same way). mainspring.py --nivaflex solves its states over the wind as for the model's spring (mainspring_t0.386.js, beside the tool: the page still draws the model's), and mainspring.py --bound finds, for both strips, where the coils pack solid to a twentieth of a turn: the states past the last solved one, each solved warm from it and from an even spiral (the quarter-turn table alone had put the model's room at 0.13 turn). The fusee's own rule then sets the spring up (where its pull falls by the chain's lever over the turns the chain takes to leave the fusee; checked to reproduce the page's full wind for the model's spring), mainspring_friction.py --nivaflex works the coils' friction, and the swing over the wind is the escapement's (ampAt) at the fusee wheel's torque running against its mean.

The model's strip against the sources' proportions

Why. The room in turns depends only on the spring's proportions, not on its size: the packed coils' pitch, the core and the length against the barrel's inside radius. The sources give exactly those ratios without needing the barrel in millimetres: the videos' packed coils against the barrel's inside edge (KLUwI2UUCMQ 15:54 and 33:06, 1/41.0-1/41.8 of the inside radius; TN24H 3SFDplGq6vs 0:16, 1/43.3-1/43.7), the length over the pitch from the packs' area (2,630-2,790), the core at 0.30-0.34 of the inside radius; and the British Museum's Model 21 (Randall and Good 1990, cat. 117A: strip 0.44 mm, barrel 38 mm inside, arbor 13 mm), whose strip is 1/43.2 of its inside radius and its core 0.34. Method. Each set of proportions is put in the model's barrel (16.3 inside) with the model's free curve and fusee and solved as above. The pitch the videos measure is the coils' spacing packed, the strip and what lies between: the solver keeps packed coils its film apart (none now; 0.01 mm until 12 October 2026), so a strip matching a pitch is the pitch less the film. Until 12 October 2026 the model took the videos' pitch, 0.394, as its strip and kept the film on top, so it packed at 0.404, 1/40.3 of the inside radius: coarser than every source. It now packs at the pitch (the strip 0.394, no film: no source gives one, and the pitch is the strip at most). The texts agree on what a spring wants: the arbor a third of the barrel's inside (Britten, Watch and Clockmakers' Handbook, 1896, p. 229: "for watches and 2-day marine chronometers"), the strip 1/30 of the arbor's diameter for chronometers (Rozé, in Saunier, Traité d'horlogerie moderne, 1887, §1214), and for a fusee about half a turn spare past full wind (Britten); turns in a real barrel are "always less" than the ideal (J. and H. Grossmann, Lessons in Horology, 1905, §§105-111), which the solve includes.

  • What it means for the build.
  • What it leaves out. The strip's edges rubbing on the barrel's floor and cap, and the steel's own hysteresis, which add to it; the ring equations take each packed coil as round, its pressure even round it. Mainsprings measured run 10-20 % under their winding (ICMFF9), which the coils alone don't reach.

11. Every mechanism, each in turn

The sections above follow the going train and the escapement. This one takes the rest of the movement's mechanisms in turn: the key and the winding stop, the ratchets and their pawls, the sustaining spring, the motion work and the hands, wear at every sliding contact, the train in time behind the stopping and starting escape wheel, and the whole movement made to a maker's tolerances at once. Each is its own tool; the figures here are those tools' own (mechdata.js).

Their geometry. mechanisms.py reads off the built model what its constants don't give: each pawl's contact on its ratchet, found by turning the ratchet the way its load turns it until its teeth first reach into the pawl's outline (only a contact that grows counts, since a drawn outline may sit a few micrometres into a tooth's back; the load's way is the one whose first contact falls on a face or a tooth's tip corner), the pawls' masses and inertias from their outlines, and every arbor's moment of inertia from its solids at their densities (tools/mechanisms.json).

11.1 The key forced

Method. What a hand puts on the key's paddle ( mm wide): finger-and-thumb torque on small handles, men tightening flanged handles 9.5 and 19 mm wide (Imrhan and Munoz-Vasa 2013) interpolated to the paddle, and Swain's 95th-percentile men on a 12.7 mm knob (1970, in the same paper); the larger is taken as a strong hand's. Wound against the stop, the stop-bar and its stud take the key's torque less the chain's (the stop holds the fusee, so the chain keeps the mainspring's pull); turned the wrong way, the key's torque adds to the chain's through the winding pawls and the sustaining spring into the train, which the escapement holds still. Each part's stress against its material's range, as strength.js does it, and the key torque at which it reaches yield. overwind.js.

  • What it means for the build.
  • What it leaves out. The stop-bar's and stud's temper (not given: hardened is the build's), a hand's dynamic jerk beyond the winding speeds taken (one and two turns a second), and the fusee arbor's own pivots under the stop's side load (strength.js's margins cover twice it).

11.2 The ratchets and their pawls

Method. Each pawl as the model seats it under load. The line of action through the contact against the pawl's pivot gives the draw: a load drawing the pawl in seats it whenever the draw angle exceeds the friction angle (μ 0.10-0.20, oiled steel). The force is the ratchet's torque over the line's arm about its centre; the tooth is bent at its root as a triangle on 0.97 of the pitch, the face's bearing taken over the pawl's depth in the teeth (a fitted end), the pawl bent by its pivot and its pivot sheared. Each spring as a cantilever (3 E I / L³): the force it adds as the pawl rides over a tip, and, for the winding pawls, the force they need to follow the fusee's teeth down when the key lets go and the mainspring turns the fusee forward under them. ratchets.js.

  • What it means for the build.
  • What it leaves out. The springs' set when free (how far a band's end stands past the pawl's back), which no source gives: the analysis gives the least set each needs, and the bench's test sets it. The setup ratchet's faces, measured undercut 3-6°, are drawn radial; their undercut would only add draw.

11.3 The sustaining spring

Method. The spring's stiffness from its section: a band bent in its plane between the pins that hold its two lobes (each pinned once, so the band is loaded by a pair of equal forces along the line through the pins), by Castigliano, (F / E I) ∫ h² ds over the free band, h a point's distance from the pins' line. Then, the key held, the train runs on it, the fusee wheel turning at its rate and the spring opening, the balance's swing following its equation (A² towards ampAt(drive)², the free balance's TF) and the escapement's rate at that swing. Since 14 October 2026 the spring is a split ring weaker than the drive, as the period's chronometers had it (Gould, 1923; Glasgow, 1885): the fusee's pull holds it closed on its banking in running, and when the key takes the fusee it pushes what its section gives at its measured 20° gap, falling as it opens (the model's SPU, which this checks against the section). The manual's "enough power to drive the chronometer for five to ten minutes" (Sec. IV) is read as the time it keeps the balance over a turn. sustaining.js.

  • Designed. (OPEN-QUESTIONS.md, "Closed by design").

11.4 The motion work and the hands

Method. The motion work's two meshes and the wind indicator's, tooth by tooth on the model's own outlines as gearing.js works the going train; the hands' play from their meshes' backlash; and the cannon pinion's friction on the centre arbor (Op. 58), between what a knock asks (the hands' out-of-balance, off the built model, at the 45 g that already frees the escape wheel) and what setting the hands forward allows (the slipping torque adds to the train's drive, and must not swing the balance to the trip, past a full turn, within a minute's setting). motionwork.js.

11.5 Wear

Method. Every sliding contact by Archard's law (the volume worn is k F s / H: the wear coefficient, the load, the distance slid, the softer part's hardness; Archard 1953), k from Rabinowicz's table for lubricated metals: about 10⁻⁷ well oiled, 10⁻⁶ poorly, 10⁻⁵ once the oil has gone. Each depth a year against the tolerance it eats: a pivot hole worn open moves its arbor toward the load and changes its meshes' depth (each mesh's close side less the hole tolerance and shake: gearing.json); the balance staff's pivot ends wear the endshake; the escape teeth's tips and the escape arbor's pivot wear the depth of lock toward its 3.9° floor (tripping.json). wear.js.

11.6 The train in time

Method. The train's inertia at the escape wheel from the solids (each arbor with what it carries), and dynamics.js run again with it. Then the train as five bodies on four meshes, each a spring (the teeth as cantilevers, estimated) with its backlash, driven at the fusee's even torque, the escape wheel moved as dynamics.js's escapement moves it through a beat: locked, let go, catching the impulse jewel and carried at its speed while its drive pushes it, let go, landing on the stone. traindyn.js.

11.7 The whole movement to a maker's tolerances

Method. Movements made with every tolerance drawn at once (the holes, the meshes' friction, the mainspring's and the escapement springs' strips, the sustaining spring's band, the pawls' springs' set), each judged on all of the above: the train turns, the balance swings in the manual's band, the maintaining work drives five minutes, the winding pawls follow the fusee down and the sustaining pawl's wire presses it in (each pawl against its own need, 11.2). Judged first as made, then after the bench (section 5's tests, by bench.json's own figures) as a maker would apply it: the depths test re-bushes a mesh closer than its window less the pivots' shake (benchcheck.py), the pawls' test bends a spring under its least set, or a wire that leaves its pawl loose, back to the aim, the swing test has the set-up turned where the swing is out of the band (Op. 13), and the maintaining work is timed (nothing on the bench re-makes the sustaining spring, so a short one stays failed, but caught). A movement still failing after the bench must have been caught by one of its tests: one that isn't means the bench's figures fall short of the analyses', and the check fails. montecarlo.js.

  • Estimated, here. The hand's torque on this key (from handles of like size), the teeth's stiffness, the meshes' and the contacts' damping, the wear coefficients (orders of magnitude), the sliding at the escape teeth and the chain, the tolerances a maker holds on the strips and springs, and the springs' set. What only a made chronometer settles is on the bench's tests (section 5).

12. The estimates swept

Method. Five design values rest on estimates no source settles: the balance's free run-down, the detent's springs' thicknesses, the discharge jewel's back radius, the sustaining spring's band and lobes, and the hairspring's upper terminal curve. Each is swept over the range its sources allow, and where the bench has a correction for it (the run-down test choosing the strip, the set-up, the ring test, the adjusting screws and the trip spring's tip, the maintaining test) it is applied as a maker would, to show that a chronometer made to the model works whichever value the real part has. estimates.js.

    What only the bench can settle

    • The mainspring. Its thickness isn't in any source (OPEN-QUESTIONS.md 1); the model's 0.394 mm is the coils' spacing on videos. It works near its material's limit (margin 1.1–1.2), as mainsprings do. Bench test 1.
    • The escapement as made. With a careful hand's tolerances, about one escapement in five runs only after adjusting (errorbudget.js, a Monte Carlo of makeEsc); the manual's Ops. 84–97 are that work. Bench test 6.
    • The hairspring's temperature coefficient. Set by the alloy's heat treatment; only the running chronometer at 55, 72½ and 90 °F shows it. The Navy test.
    • Day-to-day scatter and drift. The error budget takes them from a real Model 21's test card; a made chronometer has its own. The rating trial.
    • The estimates. The tooth form (BS 978), the pivots' shake, the detent's and trip spring's sections, the brass and steel grades are the model's estimates, listed in its README's "Estimated, not from the manual".
