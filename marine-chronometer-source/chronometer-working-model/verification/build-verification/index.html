Marine Chronometer model · verification for a build · 12 October 2026 · branch claude/build-verify

Would a chronometer made to the model run and keep time?

The aim has changed from parity with the 1948 Hamilton Model 21 to a working marine chronometer, made with a CNC and a 3D printer, steady enough to navigate by. The model already agreed with the manual and with recordings of real Model 21s; this report covers the checks added to answer the maker's question: will the gears run when the holes are bored by a machine, will it hold together and stay in adjustment from cold to hot, does the hairspring do what its design says, and how will the made chronometer be tested and rated. Every figure here is computed by a script in the repository, named beside it, from the model's own geometry and numbers.

Approach

A chronometer keeps time if three things hold: its train delivers a steady torque to the escapement (so the balance's swing, and with it the rate, stays put), its geometry stays in adjustment under load and temperature, and its balance and hairspring oscillate at a rate that changes little with swing and temperature. The model's existing checks cover the escapement in detail (its figures against the manual, its motion in time, the energy budget, the stresses and an error budget for a voyage). Nine checks were added for the build:

Units: millimetres and micrometres (µm), newtons, seconds a day (s/d, + gaining). The swing is the balance's amplitude, each way; the manual asks for 1⅜–1½ turns, 247.5–270°, and the model runs at 255°. A rate of 4 s of time is a mile of longitude at the equator.

1. The gears, tooth by tooth

The going train is four meshes: the fusee wheel drives the centre pinion, then centre → third → fourth → escape pinion. The model draws its teeth to BS 978 Part 2's clock proportions (cycloidal wheel teeth: an epicycloidal addendum, radial flanks; pinion leaves with round tips), which the manual doesn't give and the model estimates. gearing.js takes those outlines from the model's own code (core.js gearOutline), sampled finer (400 points an addendum, 240 a tip), and places wheel and pinion at their centre distance m(z₁+z₂)/2. For each of 120 steps through one pitch of the wheel it turns the pinion back against its load until the outlines touch (bisection to well under a micrometre), takes the contact's normal from the touching outline and the slip from the two bodies' velocities, and so the torque the pinion receives against the wheel's, with tooth friction µ (0.10–0.20: hardened steel on brass, unlubricated, the range physics.js uses). A ratio of 1 is a perfect, frictionless mesh.

None of the four meshes drops (the next tooth always engages before the last lets go), about a fifth of the action is before the line of centres, and the mean loss is 2–5 % a mesh. The escape wheel's torque is the product of the four ratios as their wheels turn (the centre once an hour, the fusee wheel once in 6.4 h); the balance's swing follows it through the escapement's own equation (shared/escapement.js: A² relaxes towards ampAt(drive)² with the balance's free run-down time TF = 68 s), and its rate through rateAt.

The ripple moves the rate by a few hundredths of a second a day for minutes at a time and repeats every day, so a navigator's daily rate takes it in; the time it wanders about its mean is . What matters is that the swing never leaves the band.

The depthing window and the CNC's tolerance

Each mesh's centres were then moved, the others left as drawn, until one of three things happened: it jams (the wheel's tips reach the pinion's root, or no backlash is left), its extra friction takes the day's mean swing out of the band with the set-up as drawn, or, the set-up restoring the mean (Op. 13), its ripple takes the swing out at some moment.

Each arbor has two holes, each off by up to t in any direction, and its pivots shake about 10 µm in their holes (estimated: 1–2 % of a jewelled pivot's diameter), so a mesh's centres close by at most 2t + 10 µm. Aimed at the drawn centres (BS 978's depth, kept for the teeth's sake: wider would leave less engagement for wear and knocks), the tightest mesh sets t = ± µm: a good CNC holds it with the holes bored, not drilled, and checked with gauge pins. Every mesh takes several times as much error wide as close, so a hole that errs should err wide.

2. From 40 to 100 °F

The movement is brass where it holds things (plates, bridges, pillars, wheels) and steel where it turns or locks (pinions, arbors, escape wheel, roller, detent). Brass grows × 10⁻⁶ a °C more than hardened steel (published ranges: leaded clock brass 18.7–20.9, carbon steel 10.8–12.5). From 20 °C, where the movement is put together, to 40 °F and 100 °F (wider than the Navy test's 55–90 °F, for a cabin at sea):

A mesh's depth changes only by the pinion's radius times the difference, because the wheel and its hole are both brass. In the escapement, the balance's and the detent foot's places are brass-held and the wheel, roller and detent are steel; makeEsc rebuilt with those distances grown moves lock and drop a tenth of a degree and the rate a few hundredths of a second a day. The temperature error is the balance's and its spring's alone, which is measured, not computed (section 5).

3. Finite elements on the plates and bridges

The largest loads in the movement are the fusee chain's pull (the mainspring's moment on the barrel's pitch radius, physics.js) and the first mesh's tooth force. fea.py takes the six parts that hold pivots from the page itself (each part's solid, closed and consistently wound, solids.py): the pillar plate, the train, barrel, escape and lower bridges, and the bridge on the dial side carrying the third's and fourth's lower jewels. A tetrahedral mesher (gmsh) was tried first; it found self-overlapping facets in the page's surfaces, so each solid is instead cut into voxel bricks (a ray up each column finding where it is inside), 0.25–0.55 mm in plan and 6–14 layers through the thickness. Trilinear bricks, brass at E = 100 GPa (97–110), ν = 0.34, assembled in numpy and solved by conjugate gradients preconditioned with smoothed-aggregation multigrid (pyamg), the six rigid-body modes given.

Loads, every stage lossless (the largest): the chain's pull along the external tangent of the fusee and barrel at its height on them (fully wound on the fusee's top turn, run down on its bottom), and at each mesh the tooth force, tangential from the torque and radial from the mesh's mean pressure angle (section 1, 9–11°). Each is split between its arbor's two bearings by the lever rule; a pivot's load goes on its hole's wall over the half it bears on, as cos θ. Supports: a bridge is held where it is screwed (every node within the screw head's radius, through its thickness); the pillar plate at its rim, where the case's seat ring takes it. Assembly by superposition: each fixing's reaction is put on what is beneath it (another part, or a pillar, a 6.7 mm brass rod fixed at both ends, bending as a beam); each part then follows its fixings as a rigid body fitted in plan, plus its own bending. The run is repeated with coarser bricks as a convergence check.

The plates and bridges are many times stiffer than they need to be: the hole positions and the depthing are what the maker must hold, not the parts' stiffness.

4. The hairspring, by a second method

The model's hairspring is designed, not drawn by eye (shared/hairspring.js): a helix of 12 turns, r 5.1 mm, with terminal curves solved to Phillips' conditions (1861) so its pull on the balance is a pure couple, and checked at large swings as an inextensible planar elastica solved by shooting (hairspring.js). hairspring-fe.js solves the same path a different way: co-rotational beam elements (Crisfield 1991, ch. 7; each element straight between its nodes, axial and bending stiffness, the tangent consistent), the strip extensible (0.221 mm thick: EA/EI = 12/t²), the stud's end clamped and the collet's end turned about the staff in 2.5° steps to ±300°, each step to equilibrium by Newton's method with a block-tridiagonal factorization.

The two agree on the couple's shape to a few parts in 10⁷ and on the force to two figures; their absolute stiffnesses differ by 5 × 10⁻⁴, the elements' chords being shorter than the curve. The spring's own isochronism, the rate it gives across the manual's band, is . What neither holds: the strip's own mass, and the elastic modulus's change with temperature, which is the spring maker's to set and the bench's to measure.

5. Tests on the bench

The analysis says the design works; only tests on the made parts say a chronometer made to it does. bench.js turns the model's figures into tests with pass figures; makers.py puts each on its part's card (its maker's sheet) and the whole list in the build book's last chapter (Making it → Build book). benchcheck.py judges the maker's measurements against the same figures (a JSON file it makes to fill in) and says what to do where one fails: the set-up turns that bring the mainspring into its band or even the fusee (from the curve's slope and the fusee's 1.96:1 profile), the timing weights or the millimetres to take at the stud, the manual's operation that sets an escapement figure. Its --demo plants eight faults in a made-up set and finds all eight.

6. The rating trial

The made chronometer is rated as the Navy rated the Model 21 (Sec. IX: thirty days in six periods of five, at 90, 72½, 55, 55, 72½ and 90 °F, compared daily with a time signal). rating.py reads the maker's log (the time, the chronometer's error, the temperature) and works the test's figures exactly as the model's test card does (app.js testCalc), fits the daily rates as a constant, a linear drift and a temperature curve with the rest as day-to-day scatter, backtests a ten-day rate carried on through the log itself, and projects 2000 voyages. Below it is run on a made-up log with known properties (the error budget's chronometer like No. 3390: rate +0.35 s/d, scatter 0.042 s/d, a temperature curve), sixty days, the first thirty the Navy's periods.

The fit recovers what the log was made with (the rate to 0.01 s/d, the scatter to 0.002), so on a real log its figures can be trusted as far as the log goes. Good enough to navigate by is taken as 95 % of projected voyages under 30 s (7½ miles) after 30 days.

7. The build's deviations from the Model 21

The checks above found nothing that needs changing for the chronometer to work; two changes, both with 1950s materials, make it easier to make and steadier to keep. Each is on its part's card in the page as the build's, with the Model 21's original beside it and a button back to it (the link keeps orig= for one set back); the 3D model stays the Model 21. tools/deviations.js works their numbers and writes deviations.json, which makers.py puts on the sheets.

Glucydur's own density and expansion aren't published; beryllium bronze's (CuBe2, C17200) are used. Nivarox's expansion (7–10 × 10⁻⁶/°C) and modulus (165–195 GPa) are estimated, as Elinvar-type alloys'. The spring's thermoelastic coefficient is what the spring maker grades; the temperature test settles the pair.

8. The detent under shock: does a tooth ever get past?

The classic way a detent escapement fails at sea is tripping: the escape wheel lets a tooth go without the balance having unlocked it, and the chronometer gains a beat. The detent is the weak point: a thin blade on a thinner spring, held on its stop by a preload of a few millinewtons and nothing else, with the locking stone at its end. dynamics.js takes it as one rigid body turning about the root of its spring; that answers whether it is home in time, but not whether the blade, still ringing from its landing or from the tooth's lock, lifts the stone off the next tooth, nor how hard a knock must be once its own bending is counted. tripping.js takes the detent as what it is.

Method. The detent becomes a plane frame (): beam elements with stretching, bending and their consistent mass for the two-strip spring, the blade, the arm out to the horn and the trip spring, and rigid bodies for the cross-piece with its bracket, the jewel block with the stone, and the horn; every size is the drawn part's (movement.js, ESC.pieces), every material dynamics.js's (beryllium copper 128 GPa, Elinvar-type 180 GPa). Its modes, clamped at the foot, carry it in time with the balance and the escape wheel as dynamics.js does, but every contact is elastic: the discharge jewel's own outline on the trip spring's tip (pushing it onto the horn on the active swing, bending it away on the return), the trip spring on the horn, the blade on its stop button, the tooth on the locking stone (the stone's outline and draw from makeEsc, so the tooth is pushed back as the stone lifts) and on the impulse jewel. A contact is a stiff spring with a dashpot set for its restitution (penalty contact). Op. 78 sets the preload: the spring is bent so a 0.770 g weight on the locking jewel just parts the blade from its button.

Validated. Made rigid and hinged at the spring's root, with the draw counted as dynamics.js counts it, the frame is dynamics.js's detent (stiffness, preload and first mode to 0.1 %) and runs as dynamics.js does: . Two things the frame finds that the rigid model leaves out:

  • The spring turns about its middle, not its root. A 3.8 mm spring under an 11 mm lever bends along its length, so the detent is stiffer at the stone than dynamics.js's hinge, and its first mode is Hz rather than . Running, that changes (the detent leaves the jewel and lands sooner): a constant the timing screws take up. The preload, which Op. 78 sets by weight, is the same either way.
  • The wheel recoils with its own inertia. dynamics.js holds the wheel still while the stone lifts and counts the draw as a steady moment. In the frame the jewel's strike starts the stone at once, the stone's 10° of draw throws the wheel back at 2–3 rad/s, and the tooth catches the stone up a few times before the release. Against the steady moment that changes : the averaged draw holds.
  • The tooth is free before makeEsc lets it go. makeEsc's recoil (the wheel's turn back as the stone lifts) peaks where the tooth's tip reaches the flat's inner end, the depth of lock, µm at the stone; past it the tip rides round the stone's rounded edge, where its pressure pushes the stone out, and it is free at µm. makeEsc keeps lifting to µm as room to spare and releases there. The frame lets the tooth go where it is free (), and measures every margin below against the depth of lock: a held tooth whose stone is lifted past it throws the stone off.

The largest lift over 20 beats is a maximum of a chaotic chatter: two runs a hair apart differ by about ten points. The figures are margins, not sharp limits; the lock's floor is where the margin falls below two, and the spring's band is the drawn spring to the thickest the swing allows ().

9. The box and gimbals in a seaway

A chronometer at sea hangs in gimbals so that the balance's staff stays upright whatever the ship does. The checks above took that as given. Here it is worked out: does the gimballed case keep the movement level, can it be set swinging by the ship or by the balance's own beat, and what reaches the escapement?

Method. The case with the movement in it and the gimbal ring are weighed on the model (massprops.py: every solid integrated at its material's density, for mass, centre of gravity and inertia about the gimbal centre), then run as two rigid bodies on their pivots, the ring on the box's at 3 and 9 and the case on the ring's at 6 and 12 (gimbals.js: Newton-Euler about the gimbal centre, which moves with the box; RK4). The ship's roll, pitch, yaw and heave are narrow-band random about each motion's period, the box some metres above the ship's centre of motion. Merchant ships roll at periods of 10-30 s (MARIN; Jachowski et al.); NORDFORSK's operability limit puts the box's athwartships acceleration at 0.12 g rms at the bridge, and one sea is set to it. The other levels, a small vessel's motion and the box's place in the ship are estimated. What the escapement feels, the force in its plane and the turn about the staff, is run through dynamics.js for 100 s against still water.

  • Level it. As drawn the case's pivots aren't over its centre of gravity, so it would hang out of level. The manual levels a chronometer when it first goes into its gimbals "by adjusting the position of the case brackets and gimbal support straps" (p. 13); the made one must be levelled the same way, and the analysis above assumes it is.
  • What it leaves out. The box's inside, which sets where the case meets it (), is estimated, as are the pivots' damping, the blows' stiffness and restitution, and the box's place in the ship.

10. The mainspring's coil friction

A mainspring takes more torque to wind than it gives back running: its coils slide on each other as it winds and unwinds, and rub. The energy budget had allowed for it as a published 5-15 %, the largest number in it taken on trust. Here it is worked out on the model's own spring.

Method. mainspring.py solves the strip's shape at every quarter turn of the wind as the one of least bending energy, its coils kept apart but frictionless (js/mainspring.js). Each of those states is polished by the same solve (mainspring_friction.py). The slip follows the steel: the two pieces that touch, on a coil and on the one over it, are followed from one state to the next, and the difference of their movements along the strip is how far they slide on each other; on the barrel's wall against the barrel's turn, on the core against the arbor's. The pressure between packed coils comes from the packed spiral's ring equations: each coil a ring bent by its own moment, EI(k − k₀), the torque the same at every section, so each coil's hoop force is (τ − M)/r, and each ring's radial balance passes the pressure on to the next from the pack's free edge (the solver's own multipliers and the rod's equations by differentiation were tried first, and failed their own checks in the packs: the second derivatives of a sampled shape are too rough). The friction's work, μ times pressure times slip, over each quarter turn is a torque that adds to winding and comes off running. μ: greased steel on steel, 0.08-0.2 lubricated (the CRC Handbook, as cited in US 3,939,081); 0.10 taken, 0.08 and 0.16 tried.

  • What it means for the build.
  • What it leaves out. The strip's edges rubbing on the barrel's floor and cap, and the steel's own hysteresis, which add to it; the ring equations take each packed coil as round, its pressure even round it. Mainsprings measured run 10-20 % under their winding (ICMFF9), which the coils alone don't reach.

What only the bench can settle

  • The mainspring. Its thickness isn't in any source (OPEN-QUESTIONS.md 1); the model's 0.394 mm is the coils' spacing on videos. It works near its material's limit (margin 1.1–1.2), as mainsprings do. Bench test 1.
  • The escapement as made. With a careful hand's tolerances, about one escapement in five runs only after adjusting (errorbudget.js, a Monte Carlo of makeEsc); the manual's Ops. 84–97 are that work. Bench test 6.
  • The hairspring's temperature coefficient. Set by the alloy's heat treatment; only the running chronometer at 55, 72½ and 90 °F shows it. The Navy test.
  • Day-to-day scatter and drift. The error budget takes them from a real Model 21's test card; a made chronometer has its own. The rating trial.
  • The estimates. The tooth form (BS 978), the pivots' shake, the detent's and trip spring's sections, the brass and steel grades are the model's estimates, listed in its README's "Estimated, not from the manual".
