Winding

The fusee goes 56 hours on a wind, 8¾ of its turns; the barrel turns about 5 times. While the key turns the fusee back, its clicks ride over the click wheel and the maintaining detent holds the ratchet, so the maintaining spring keeps the train going for about a minute. Run down, the train stops and the balance swings down to rest: a detent escapement doesn't start itself, so once wound it needs a twist to set the balance swinging again. The swing comes from the fusee's pull, measured on the drawings, through the train; the rate shown is the escapement's, against its own at 12 hours from full wind.

A chronometer built from its drawings. Wempe’s works drawings for the German standard marine chronometer give each hole’s place to a thousandth of a millimetre, and beside them a measurement its makers took. This essay reads them, and shows where the model on the other tab finds them agreeing, and where it doesn’t.

A companion to The Marine Chronometer, the essay on the Hamilton Model 21, which explains how a chronometer keeps time: this one links to it where an idea is the same. The drawings themselves are on the Deutsche Gesellschaft für Chronometrie’s site; every figure here is drawn from the model’s own numbers. Sections on the hairspring, the balance in heat and cold, and the gimbals are to come.

One standard chronometer

The Deutsches Einheits-Chronometer, the German standard chronometer, was designed by Gerhard D. Wempe’s chronometer works in Hamburg in the early 1940s. It was made by Wempe and by A. Lange & Söhne during the war, and by Wempe after it. It is a marine chronometer of the classic kind: a fusee and chain, a spring detent escapement, a cut bimetallic balance with a helical spring, in a brass bowl hung in gimbals in a wooden box.

The Deutsche Gesellschaft für Chronometrie’s page that publishes its drawings names Ernst William Meier and Friedrich Leutert as its geistige Väter, its intellectual fathers, and John Schwarzer and Herbert Müller as its chief designers, whose names are in the drawings’ title blocks. Several hundred drawings were made, and the wartime ones were revised from what series production taught, the balance staff and the escape wheel among them. The published set comes from Wempe, from private owners and from the Deutsches Uhrenmuseum Glashütte, which holds copies stamped “KOPIE” of the drawings Wempe supplied to Lange.

One date doesn’t fit. The escapement’s layout, “Hemmung für Feder-Chronometer, Kaliber 020” (sheet 05 04_1), is dated 03.02.40, which the page reads as 1941, since Wempe had no spring chronometer in 1940. The lever chronometer’s escapement sheet, 05 04_20, is dated 03.02.41.

Knirim and Schmiedchen (watch-wiki) list changes made at Glashütte: the bowl and gimbal ring in Bakelite, with a heavy weight in the bottom, from No. 5100; the balance lowered to 3.8 mm from No. 5111; the maintaining spring (Gegenfeder) redesigned at Lange from No. 5201. Lange No. 5177, of 1944, is photographed on dg-chrono.de with its papers.

The model on the other tab is the spring-detent version, the Feder-Chronometer, Kaliber 05, as its drawings draw it, each sheet as last revised. Where a sheet gives a figure, the model uses it; where it doesn’t, the size is measured on the drawing at its scale or on photographs of real ones, and marked as estimated. Each part’s card says which.

Reading a works drawing

A works drawing is a sheet for the shop: one part, drawn to a scale, with every size it must be made to and how closely, its material, and who drew and checked it. Take the pillar, 05 08 00-1, one of the three that hold the plates apart. Herbert Müller drew it on 2 June 1940, at five times full size, in Rundmessing 8,5 Din 1758 Ms 58, round brass bar 8.5 mm across. Its body is Ø 8.1 −0.2. It stands 19.6 ±0.01 between its shoulders, and at each end a spigot Ø 4.5 f7, 2.8 long, goes into a plate. The sheet also gives the overall length, 25.3 −0.2, which the parts check: 19.6 + 2 × 2.8 = 25.2. The pillar is tapped M2.6 right through for the pillar screws, “Gewinde von beiden Seiten einschneiden”: cut the thread from both ends.

“f7” is a tolerance from the standard system of fits: for a shaft 4.5 across it means between 0.010 and 0.022 mm under size, 4.478 to 4.490. The plates’ holes for the spigots are reamed “4,5 +0,012”, 4.500 to 4.512. So a spigot always slides in, with 0.010 mm to 0.034 mm to spare across its hole: the pillars set where the top plate lies over the pillar plate, and so where each arbor’s upper pivot hole stands over its lower one.

The shoulders don’t sit on the plates’ faces but in seats, Ø 8.6 and 0.3 +0.03 deep, turned into each plate’s inner face. So the plates stand 19.6 − 2 × 0.3 = 19.0 mm apart, the “innerer Plattenabstand 19 mm” of the overall plan (sheet 001), and 18.93 to 19.01 at the limits.

The plates come with tables. The top plate’s sheet, 05 09 01, lists every hole by its number, with its place as two coordinates from hole 1, the centre wheel’s arbor, at the plate’s centre: N or S, and O (Ost, east) or W. Then how each is made: centre-punched (Körner) or punched to a size, drilled, then reamed or tapped. The pillars are holes 11, 39 and 49; hole 11 is “S 3,225 O 36,858”, punched 4.3 and reamed 4.5 +0.012. Worked out, the three lie on one circle 37.0 mm about the centre, at 95°, 210° and 350° from N toward O: 115°, 140° and 105° apart, not evenly spaced. A hole has the same number and the same coordinates on every sheet it appears on, the plates, the cocks and the bridges; read across them all, they agree to the last figure.

The fusee, measured by its makers

A mainspring pulls harder fully wound than run down, and the fusee, a grooved cone the chain winds onto, gives the train an even pull from it. Wempe’s drawings include a record of how even. Sheet 05 06_20, “Schneckenkurve / Kraftverlauf” (the fusee’s curve, the course of the force), signed by Herbert Müller on 24 July 1941, plots the force over the fusee’s turns: “Mittel aus 2 Messungen”, the mean of two measurements, with a lever 262 mm long (“Hebellänge 262 m/m”), running down (“Ablauf”). Over the whole wind it reads between 126.5 and 138.4 g; the sheet’s own mean is 131.5 g.

Where was the lever? The sheet works out its own sum, 131.5 × 262 / 19.6 = 1.758 kg at the fusee wheel’s pitch circle (d = 39.202), which takes no account of the fusee’s radius; and a pull as even as this over the barrel’s five turns is what a fusee hands its arbor, not what a barrel gives. So the model reads it as measured on the fusee arbor, with the spring’s, the chain’s and the fusee’s losses already in it. Through the going train, 3,085.7 to 1 from the fusee wheel to the escape pinion, at an efficiency the model estimates at 0.749 (0.661–0.848 from its pivots and teeth), that is 78.9–86.3 µN·m at the escape wheel over the wind.

No sheet gives the mainspring’s length, only its strip, 15 × 0.37 mm, on the same sheet. The model solves the spring as the Hamilton’s is solved: an elastic strip coiled in the drawn barrel, its shape the one of least bending energy at each quarter turn of the wind, its coils pressing on each other, set up three quarters of a turn (“3/4 Umgang gespannt”) and wound through the drawn fusee. A strip 1,240 mm long gives the measured force’s shape to 1.07 % rms: the hump a turn from run down, the dip in the middle, the rise to full wind.

The level is another matter. At 0.37 mm the solved strip pulls 25 % harder than the sheet measured: the measured force is 0.802 of its pull without friction. The losses between the spring and the fusee arbor, worked out from the parts (the coils rubbing, the barrel on its arbor, the chain’s links on their rivets, the fusee’s pivots), come to 3.5–14.6 %; the measurement asks for 13.6–24.1 %. It closes only with the losses at the top of their range, or with a strip about 0.355 mm thick. And solved at 0.37 mm the strip would be stressed 2.73 GPa fully wound, past a steel mainspring’s limit. The sheet’s “0,37” has its 7 written over another figure.

The escapement in time

The spring detent escapement locks the escape wheel on a stone set in the detent, a light arm on a spring. Once each oscillation, the discharge stone on the balance’s roller lifts the detent by its gold spring and lets a tooth go; the wheel turns, drives the balance through the impulse stone on the other roller, and the next tooth locks. On the swing back the discharge stone only bends the gold spring aside.

Wempe’s layout, 05 04_1, draws the escapement in angles. At rest the impulse stone’s face stands 24° before the line of centres, the line through the balance’s and the wheel’s arbors, and the locked tooth is 36.5° round the wheel from it. The layout also draws the tooth falling on the impulse stone 21° before the line of centres, as if the wheel were there the moment it was let go: that is how the model reads the sheet’s “21°”.

A wheel let go from rest can’t do that. Solved from the parts’ shapes and masses (the escapement solver the Hamilton’s model uses, with the Wempe’s settings), the gold spring frees the tooth with the balance at −2.4° (the lock is 9.9° of swing, the let-off 1.9°), and the impulse stone comes into the teeth’s path 4.6° later. The wheel starts from rest, with the seconds arbor behind it, on only 82.8 µN·m, and gathers speed slowly. It strikes the stone 17.2° of swing after it was let go, the stone’s face 9.2° before the line of centres, and drives it to 21.8° past. The strike costs 22 % of the impulse, and the unlocking takes 18 % of its work. Integrated in time, beat by beat, with the balance, the wheel and the train behind it and the detent moving as bodies through every contact, the tooth lands where the solver puts it, within the 1.5° that check allows.

Taken in time, two more things show that a layout can’t. The detent leaves the discharge stone at −1.8°, flung up off its square corner rather than riding down its bevelled end, and the energy it carries there is lost. And the wheel, let go from rest, has hardly moved by then, so the detent falls back onto the tooth it has just let go, at 0.9°, and rides it, and down its back, until it is home at 10.3°, its friction on the tooth holding back the wheel’s chase. Both cost swing, and the model’s escapement takes both in.

The model’s balance swings 214° twelve hours after winding. That rests on an estimate: no Wempe source gives how quickly the balance loses its swing, so its free run-down time, 124 s, is estimated from its parts (the air on its masses and screws, the endstone’s friction, the spring’s own losses). The 1954 Soviet instruction for checking chronometers gives 225–270° for chronometers in general.

The reparation drawings and the 6MX

The drawings held at A. Lange & Söhne became the basis of the post-war Russian marine chronometers. Johannes Altmeppen, quoting Kurt Herkner, writes that two specialists from Moscow and an interpreter came to Glashütte in 1946/47, and the drawings were translated into Cyrillic. The 1st Moscow Watch Factory made the chronometer from them as the 6MX. Its movement has three pillars because the reparation drawings were of that type; the factory made about twenty changes, of which there are no drawings.

Altmeppen compares a Wempe with a 6MX and marks the differences. In the 6MX pillar 39 is in another place; the seconds and intermediate wheels run in jewels in settings, not in brass bearings; the barrel’s cap is underneath; the detent’s block is new, with an adjusting slide; the lower balance cock has another shape; the upper cock has no window, and the three small windows by the pillar screws are gone; the bowl and bezel are brass, not Buna; the dial’s feet are at 19, 39 and 59 minutes, not 20, 40 and 59; and there is no hand-setting from outside.

The 6MX’s technical conditions, TU 25-07.1533-84, give its movement as 85.0 mm across and 39.8 mm high, with a balance of 0.5 s, 56 hours on the wind indicator and 15 jewels, the whole at most 2.2 kg. They set the trial each chronometer had to pass: five periods of seven days, at 36, 20, 4, 20 and 36 °C, the last five days of each counted. That is the class II trial of the Soviet standard GOST 8916-58, with limits of the TU’s own between the standard’s two classes, and two more figures: the middle-temperature error S, and a figure of merit N.

The copies are evidence, not the source. Where a 6MX or a post-war Wempe differs from the drawings, the model follows the drawings.

About this model

Orel Magidish (assisted by Claude Opus 5.5) – October 2026 · version dev

A 3D model, in progress, of the Deutsches Einheits-Chronometer: the standard marine chronometer designed by Gerhard D. Wempe’s chronometer works in Hamburg in the early 1940s, made by Wempe and A. Lange & Söhne in the war, by Wempe after it, and from the same drawings by the 1st Moscow Watch Factory as the 6MX. It is the spring-detent version, with a fusee and chain and three pillars, as its works drawings draw it.

Sources

  1. Wempe’s works drawings, as published by the Deutsche Gesellschaft für Chronometrie (dg-chrono.de), “© Johannes Altmeppen (by courtesy of Gerhard D. Wempe KG) 2017”, each sheet as last revised. Each dimension in the model names its sheet.
  2. Johannes Altmeppen’s photographs of Wempe 3955 and Lange 5177 (dg-chrono.de, © 2017), for what the sheets leave open: the standard screws’ heads, finishes, which way a part turns.

How it is being made

  • From the drawings. Every size and place is read off a sheet: a figure where the sheet gives one, and where it doesn’t, measured on the drawing at its scale and marked estimated. Each part’s card says which.
  • So far. The frame (drawing group 05 08): the plates, 85 mm across and 19 mm apart on three pillars, in the movement ring, with the bridges, cocks and bushes that are screwed to them. Holes for the parts still to come are cut as the plates’ tables give them. The balance and the spring detent escapement (groups 05 03 and 05 04): the bimetallic balance on its staff, the helical hairspring, the rollers, the escape wheel, the detent with its gold spring and block, and their jewels, working as the escapement’s layout drawing lays it out. The going train (05 05). The fusee and barrel (05 06, 05 07): the fusee with its nine-turn groove, its wheel and Harrison’s maintaining work, the stop work, the barrel with its set-up ratchet and mainspring, and the chain or the band. The dial and its hands (group 05 02): the white dial with its sub-dials for the state of wind and the seconds, the motion work that turns the hour hand from the centre arbor, and the up-and-down wheel driven from the fusee. The case and box (groups 05 01 and 51 00): the cast brass bowl, its bezel and glass and its closing plate, hung in the gimbal ring in an oak box 185 mm square, the transit lock, and the one-way winding key of 1943.
