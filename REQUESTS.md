# What we'd ask a Model 21 owner or the NAWCC for

**Since 14 October 2026 none of these holds up the design** (OPEN-QUESTIONS.md, "Closed by design"): each is settled by a design value the checks pass and a bench test that makes the made part right. The answers would make the model truer to the original Model 21, not make a chronometer built to it run better; item 3 (the free run-down) is now also a bench test any maker runs on their own balance.

A post to send, for the readings no video, photograph or the 1948 manual settles (OPEN-QUESTIONS.md, `References/VIDEOS.md`). Each item says what to photograph or measure and why. A phone photograph is enough when it is square to the part and has a ruler, or a coin of known size, beside it.

**Measurements of a Hamilton Model 21 wanted for an open working model**

I'm building a working 3D model of the Hamilton Model 21 from the 1948 overhaul manual (NAVSHIPS 250-624) and teardown videos: https://www.marinechronometermodel.com. A few sizes can't be read from any source I've found. If you have a Model 21 apart for service, any one of these would help, and I'd credit you on the site.

1. **The barrel and mainspring:** the barrel's diameter outside and inside, with a caliper (the British Museum's catalogue gives 38 mm inside, which disagrees with the videos' scale); the mainspring's thickness with a micrometer, its width and, if it's out, its length (the catalogue gives 0.44 × 15.0 mm on a late example, the parts list 0.0165 in); the barrel arbor's diameter where the spring hooks (the catalogue: 13 mm).
2. **The detent:** the thickness of its spring's two strips and of the trip (passing) spring (their widths and lengths are known), or a side-on photograph of the detent out of the movement with a scale beside it.
3. **The balance's free run-down:** with the train held by the train-blocking screw and the balance given a full swing, a video of it swinging down for a minute or two, filmed square from above. (A start filmed from the twist to a steady swing helps too; a timegrapher's amplitude and beat error even more. The one start filmed so far, on one movement, builds up as if its balance ran down in 100-150 s and settles near 300°, where the model takes 67 s and 255°: a second movement would tell whether that's the Model 21 or that one.)
4. **The fusee with the chain off:** its large end, showing where the chain's hook goes into the groove's lowest turn and where the groove ends.
5. **The winding ratchet** on the fusee's large end, face-on and close, ideally with a scale: its teeth's depth.
6. **The hairspring collet** off its staff, from below, or the spring's inner end seen from under the balance: the collet's body under its sector plate.
7. **The fourth wheel** lying flat beside a ruler, its diameter over the teeth, or the distance between the fourth and escape arbors' holes on the bare plate (the third wheel is now measured).
8. **The set-up ratchet** (on the barrel arbor under its cover), off its arbor and face-on, still and in focus: whether its locking faces are radial or slightly undercut.
9. **The discharge jewel** on the balance staff's unlocking roller, looking along the staff: the shape of its tip (the British Museum's catalogue: "a radius to the back of the acting part").
10. **The fusee's sustaining ratchet with the two winding-pawl springs on**, face-on.

Doug Sinclair's NAWCC article on the Model 21 (No. 365, p. 707), William O. Bennett's "The Hamilton Marine Chronometer" (No. 356, p. 325), and the Bulletin's pages behind the members' login (Smith, No. 395, pp. 60-63), may answer 1 and 2; if you can read them, a note of the figures they give would help too. If you have Hamilton's drawings of the Model 21 (the NAWCC Library holds them in the Hamilton Records Collection, MG-335: "Chronometer Parts" 1943-1951, Box 14, Folder 6; the mainspring drawings, Box 7, Folders 7-8), the mainspring's, the barrel's and the detent's sheets would settle 1 and 2 outright.
